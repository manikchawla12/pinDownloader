We measured 23 Pinterest videos: none of them were 1080p

By PinClip Editorial • Published 9 August 2026 • 9 min read

What we did

Almost every article about Pinterest video specifications — including, until today, the one that used to sit at this URL — repeats the same numbers: “1080p Full HD”, “up to 4K”, “2GB maximum upload”. Those figures describe what Pinterest lets a creator upload. They say nothing about what Pinterest actually serves back, which is the only thing that matters when you download a pin.

So we measured it. In August 2026 we took a sample of 32 public video pins found through ordinary search across five categories (recipes, fitness, DIY and crafts, beauty, home decor), resolved each one, downloaded the highest-quality rendition available, and ran ffprobe against the resulting file. Twenty-three pins produced a video file. Every number below comes from those 23 files.

The complete raw dataset is published here so you can check our arithmetic: pinterest-video-sample-2026-08.csv.

Finding 1: nothing in the sample was 1080p

This was the surprise. Not one of the 23 videos exceeded 720 pixels on the long edge of the horizontal axis. The widest file in the entire sample was 720px wide.

The two clusters are 576×1024 (11 files) and 720×1280 (10 files). The remaining two were 720×1080 and a handful of one-offs at 486×864, 540×960 and 576×1026.

If you have read that Pinterest serves Full HD video, that claim does not survive contact with the actual files. We cannot rule out that some pins somewhere are served at 1080p — 23 is a small sample — but none of ours were, and the consistency of the 720px ceiling suggests it is a deliberate delivery cap rather than a coincidence.

Finding 2: Pinterest's own metadata overstates resolution

Five of the 23 pins advertised a resolution larger than the file they actually delivered. In each case the culprit was the same: alongside its HLS ladder, Pinterest exposes one or more progressive MP4 entries labelled V_EXP3 through V_EXP7, or a particularly misleading one called V_720P. These entries carry no codec, bitrate or frame-rate information, and the width and height they report appear to be the pin's display dimensions rather than the encoded video's.

The clearest example: a recipe pin advertised V_720P at 1080×1920. The file that arrives is 720×1280. The format is named “720P”, reports 1920px, and delivers 1280px.

The practical consequence is that any tool trusting that metadata will report a resolution it cannot deliver. We checked our own downloader against this and it selects the correct file — but it was reporting the inflated figure back to users, which we have now corrected.

Finding 3: two files had no audio at all

21 of 23 files carried an AAC audio track. Two were silent — not muted, but genuinely lacking an audio stream. Both were short clips (6.4s and 17.0s) where the creator appears to have uploaded video with no soundtrack. If a download plays silently, this is the likely explanation and there is nothing a downloader can do about it.

Finding 4: file size is roughly 7.6 MB per minute

Across the sample the median works out to 7.6 MB per minute of video, but the spread is wide — from 2.1 MB/min to 17.6 MB/min. Overall bitrate ranged from 275 kbps to 2,346 kbps with a median of 1,014 kbps.

As a rule of thumb for planning storage: a typical 20-second Pinterest video lands somewhere between 1.5 MB and 3 MB. The 145-second makeup tutorial in our sample — the longest video we measured — came to 18 MB.

Finding 5: 9:16 dominates, but not universally

19 of 23 videos were exactly 9:16. Two were 2:3, and two were irregular ratios (32:57 and 30:43) that do not correspond to any standard — almost certainly the result of a creator uploading an oddly-cropped source file that Pinterest passed through without normalising.

Frame rate was 30 fps in 15 cases and 60 fps in 3. The remainder were 24, 25, 29.92, 29.97 and 50 fps — the fingerprints of source footage shot for film or PAL broadcast and never converted.

Finding 6: roughly one pin in four cannot be downloaded

Of the 32 pins we started with, 9 produced no video file. The failures break down as:

  • 5 pins returned “no video formats found”. These are image pins that appeared in video-related search results, or pins whose video has since been removed.
  • 2 pins turned out to be Pinterest pins that embed a YouTube video rather than hosting their own. The extractor correctly followed through to YouTube, which then refused the request. This is a genuinely interesting edge case that we had not accounted for.
  • 1 pin returned a 404 — deleted between the search result being indexed and our request.

A ~72% success rate on an unfiltered sample sounds low, but the sample was deliberately unfiltered: we did not check first that each URL was a genuine, live, self-hosted video pin. If you paste a link to a video you are currently watching on Pinterest, your success rate will be far higher.

How Pinterest structures its video delivery

Every pin we measured was served through the same two-track arrangement:

  • An HLS ladder — a median of 4 video-only rungs, named V_HLSV3_MOBILE-{bitrate} or V_HLSV4-{bitrate}, with a separate audio stream alongside. A typical ladder runs 234×416 at ~395 kbps, 360×640 at ~625 kbps, 486×864 at ~878 kbps, then the top rung.
  • One or more progressive MP4s over plain HTTPS, which are self-contained (video and audio in one file) but carry no useful metadata.

Because the HLS rungs are video-only, any tool reassembling them needs ffmpeg to mux the audio back in. A downloader running without ffmpeg will either fall back to a progressive file or hand you a silent video — which is one reason the same pin can produce different results on different sites.

Limitations of this study

We want to be straight about what this does and does not show.

  • 23 files is a small sample. It is enough to disprove “Pinterest always serves 1080p” but not enough to establish precise population statistics.
  • The pins were found via search, which biases towards popular, well-indexed content. Videos from small accounts may behave differently.
  • All measurements were taken from one location (India) on one date. Pinterest's CDN may serve different renditions in different regions.
  • Pinterest can change any of this at any time. These numbers describe August 2026. We will re-run the measurement periodically and note the date when we do.

What this means for you

Practically: expect a vertical file somewhere around 576×1024 or 720×1280, H.264 video with AAC audio, roughly 1 Mbps, and a few megabytes per minute. That is perfectly good for watching on a phone and fine for most editing. It is not master-quality footage, and no downloader can make it so — a tool can only hand you the best file Pinterest is willing to serve. Anyone promising you 4K or guaranteed 1080p from a Pinterest pin is describing something we could not reproduce.
